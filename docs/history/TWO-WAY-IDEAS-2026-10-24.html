Two-way rows: working from the page you're on

Today the rows show what points at a page. To change one of those nodes, add a new one, or follow a link onward, you leave the page.

These five ideas let you do it right where you are. The example is a page about one concept you are studying, Spaced repetition. Claims, sources, observations, questions, projects and other concepts all point at it. Every idea works the same for any page, tag or property.

In each drawing, what is new has a soft orange background. Every change gets the faded Undo arrow, as today.

1. Group a row by anything

Where you are: the Spaced repetition page. Nine claims point at it, and you want to see at once which hold up and which are disputed.

  1. Click the small words after the count, "grouped by".
  2. Pick Confidence. You can also pick the tag, the source, the month, or any property the claims have.
  3. The claims show in groups. Click a group's name to fold it. The row remembers your choice.

In your notes: nothing changes. Only how the row is shown.

Where you are: the same page, claims grouped by Confidence. You have just read something that cuts against the method and want to note it as a disputed claim.

  1. Point at the Disputed group. A + appears at its end.
  2. Click it. A line opens under the group.
  3. Type the claim and press Enter.

3. Write into a node without leaving

Where you are: the same page. A claim catches your eye and you want to add a thought to it, not to the concept page.

  1. Click the small arrow before the claim. Its own bullets open under its line.
  2. Click after the last bullet and type.
  3. Click the arrow again to close it. The concept page is as it was.

In your notes: the new bullet is inside the claim, exactly as if you had typed it on the claim's own page.

4. Follow a chain without leaving

The idea in one line: the small arrow you now have before a node opens its bullets. For 4, it would also list the node's properties. Each one that links to another node gets its own small arrow, so you can keep going, one node under the next, without leaving the page.

What happens, step by step: you click the arrow before a node. Under it you see its properties as lines. A property that holds a link, such as Source or Provider, has its own arrow. You click that, and the linked node opens under it with its own properties, and so on. Reading only. Nothing is written. It stops after three steps, and it will not open a node that is already open above it, so a loop cannot run away.

Examples, on any kind of notes:

  • Illness graph. On Colon cancer, open "First appointment with my oncologist". Open its Provider, Dr. Mei Chen. Open her Works at, Northgate. You see where she works without opening three pages.
  • Research notes. On a concept page, open a claim. Open its Source, then the source's Authors. You see who stands behind the claim.
  • Projects. On a project page, open a task. Open its Owner. You see that person's other properties, such as their team.
  • Reading list. On a topic page, open a book. Open its Author, then the author's other books.

Where it falls short: if you only want one fact, resting the pointer on a node already shows its properties in a card. And a long chain gets tall. So it is a convenience, not a new ability. The part that is new is keeping the path in front of you as you go deeper.

My view: a small extra on top of what you now have. The cheapest version is a single step: the opened node also lists its properties. I would do that one step later, and skip the deeper chain unless you miss it.

5. A row for what sits two steps away

The idea in one line: a page's rows show what points straight at it. A new row would show what those nodes point at, which sits two steps away. For example, this concept page shows its claims, and the new row shows the sources those claims rest on.

What happens, step by step: you right-click a row's name and choose "Add a row through this". A list shows the properties those nodes hold, the same list the "group" menu shows. You pick one. A new row appears under the first, showing every different node that property points at, each with how many of the row's nodes point there. Click a name to open it. The choice is remembered for that row. Nothing is written, since it only reads links that already exist.

Examples, on any kind of notes:

  • Research notes. Spaced repetition, row "claims about", through Source: Cepeda 2006 (3 claims), Roediger 2011 (2), Rohrer 2015 (2). You see what the evidence rests on.
  • Illness graph, a condition. Colon cancer, row "topic of" (consultations and conversations), through Provider: Dr. Mei Chen 2, Dr. Raj Patel 1, Dr. Helen Brooks 1. You see who has been involved.
  • Illness graph, a doctor. Dr. Mei Chen, row "provider of", through Topic: Colon cancer 3, Peripheral neuropathy 1. You see what she has dealt with.
  • Illness graph, a hospital. Northgate Cancer Center, row "works at" (doctors), through Specialty: Oncology 2. You see what is practised there.
  • Projects. A project page, row "project of" (tasks), through Owner: Alex 5, Sam 3. You see who is on it.
  • People. A person page, row "attendee of" (meetings), through Project: Mobile app 4, Search 2. You see what they are part of.

Where it falls short: it only follows one property at a time, and only one step. If just some nodes hold the property, the count can mislead, so the row would say "3 of 5 have it". It cannot say why two things are linked. It is only a count of links.

My view: the more practical of the two. The reading it needs is the same one that now powers "group by", so it is cheap, and it answers a question the page cannot answer today: what do these nodes lead to? I would build this one next.
